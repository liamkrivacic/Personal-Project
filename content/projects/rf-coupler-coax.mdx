AtomCraft is a student engineering project building a kilowatt-level microwave heating system for
a plasma test vessel. The RF line carries roughly **1 kW at 2.45 GHz** down **WR340 waveguide**
from a magnetron to the plasma. To run that line safely and tune it, we need to know how much
power is moving in each direction, and we need to measure it without the calibrated slotted lines
and precision network analysers a proper RF lab would have. There are two numbers we care about:

- **Forward power** is how much energy actually reaches the load. It also lets us compare the
  waveguide sections we're manufacturing and pick the one with the lowest insertion loss.
- **Reflected power** shows how badly the line is mismatched to the plasma. The three-stub tuner
  uses it to match the load, keep the standing-wave ratio low and keep reflected power out of the
  magnetron.

<Callout title="Scope and status">
This page follows the order I did the project in: **preliminary research** to pick an
architecture, the **HFSS design** of the directional coupler and waveguide-to-coax transition,
and the **power measurement PCB** that reads it all out. The coupler and both transitions have
been manufactured but haven't been measured on a VNA yet, so their performance figures are
simulation results. The second version of the detector PCB has been built and bench-tested.
</Callout>

## Preliminary research

I started by reading PhD theses and papers from tokamak electron-cyclotron resonance heating
(ECRH) programs, ion-thruster work and microwave-plasma deposition rigs, basically anything that
measured RF power in a plasma system. Almost every method assumed equipment a student team
doesn't have: calibrated waveguide slotted lines, precision network analysers or commercial
in-line power meters. The physics was covered well, but none of them explained how to build the
measurement into a working diagnostic on a student budget.

The most useful paper was a 2024 one by Ferrand, Desangles and Elias: [**Non-intrusive
measurements of plasma impedance in an electron-cyclotron resonance thruster**](https://doi.org/10.1063/5.0191658).
Their method was something we could actually build. A **dual directional coupler** taps a known
fraction of the incident and reflected signals, and a VNA recovers the load's complex reflection
coefficient from the ratio $b_{\text{REF}}/b_{\text{FWD}}$ without interrupting the RF system. I
used it as the template for AtomCraft's diagnostic.

Based on that paper, the diagnostic needs three parts:

1. **A directional coupler** that takes off a small known fraction of the power (~40 dB down
   from 1 kW) and separates forward and reflected waves into different ports.
2. **A waveguide-to-coax transition** that gets the sampled power out of the WR340 waveguide
   and onto 50 Ω coax so it can reach the detector.
3. **A power measurement PCB** that converts the 2.45 GHz signal into a DC voltage using a
   logarithmic detector IC, which a Raspberry Pi reads.

The final system architecture:

### How the architecture changed

It took a few iterations to get to that diagram.

- **Setup 1** was a coupler, a coax transition and a tuner in series. The problem was that one
  line can't be matched to the plasma and the measurement probe at the same time. Every time the
  three-stub tuner moved to follow the plasma, it de-tuned the probe and changed the coupling
  factor.
- **Setups 2–3** worked around that. Instead of trying to hold a perfect match, I redefined the
  **coupling factor** as the ratio of waveguide power to the power actually delivered to the
  detector, so every mismatch is folded into one constant. With **two identical coupler-to-coax
  stages in series**, you can read both and solve for that constant directly, without a separate
  measurement tuner.
- **Setup 4** moved into HFSS, and that's where the design settled. I didn't build the two-stage
  version in the end. A single, well-toleranced coupler was simpler, and HFSS let me pin down the
  coupling factor in simulation, with a VNA check on the bench to follow. Working through the
  two-coupler idea is what showed me the measurement tuner was the real constraint, and that a
  tight single coupler removed it.

## Directional coupler

A directional coupler is a four-port device that samples a fixed fraction of the main-line power
into a secondary port. The key property is **directivity**: because of how the coupling
apertures add in phase, forward and reflected waves come out of different ports. That's what
lets us read reflected power on its own, which is what the three-stub tuner needs.

At WR340 and kilowatt power the options are limited. Stripline and ferrite designs can't handle
the power, so the only practical type is a **waveguide-aperture coupler** cut into the guide
wall. I looked at three geometries:

<Figure
  src="/projects/rf-coupler-coax/coupler-types.png"
  alt="CAD drawings of three waveguide directional coupler types: cross-guide, broadwall, and loop"
  caption="Three coupler geometries evaluated for WR340 at 2.45 GHz: cross-guide, broadwall, and loop coupler."
  width={714}
  height={420}
/>

The **cross-guide** geometry had the most published work behind it. It puts the coupling
apertures in the shared wall between two perpendicular waveguides, spaced λg/4 apart so the
coupled signals add in the forward direction and cancel in the reverse. Paul Wade (W1GHZ) built
and measured two versions using CNC machining: a three-hole round-aperture coupler, and an X-slot
version called a **Moreno coupler**. Both can be made on a CNC mill, which is what AtomCraft has
access to, and the round-aperture design reached 35+ dB directivity in WR-42 and WR-19. That left
two aperture shapes to choose between, round holes or crossed slots, and I used simulation to
decide:

<Figure
  src="/projects/rf-coupler-coax/moreno-schematic.png"
  alt="Moreno cross-guide coupler schematic showing X-shaped apertures, isolation port, output port, and coupling port at 45 degrees"
  caption="Moreno cross-guide coupler geometry: two X-shaped apertures spaced λg/4 apart give the directional cancellation. After Wade (W1GHZ), Waveguide Cross-guide Directional Couplers, 2019."
  width={865}

### HFSS simulation

HFSS (High Frequency Structure Simulator) solves Maxwell's equations with the finite element
method over a 3D model of the coupler and gives S-parameters across the band. I used it to sweep
the geometry (aperture cross-width, arm length, aperture separation and probe depth) to find a
shape that met the targets before cutting any metal. The targets were flat −40 dB coupling and
more than 25 dB directivity at 2.45 GHz, and the design had to stay there across realistic
machining tolerances.

The parameters all affect each other, so trying values by hand didn't get far. Instead I ran a
sequence of search-based and gradient-based optimisers. The sweep below is a typical example of
how each variable shifts the S-parameter response:

<Figure

  caption="Representative parametric sweep from the HFSS optimisation process. The resonance tracks the swept parameter but sits below 2.45 GHz, illustrating the kind of result the optimiser had to navigate away from to find the final geometry."
  width={1667}
  height={870}

/>

Starting from Wade's round apertures, the optimiser found a geometry with coupling flat at
−40 dB and directivity above 25 dB across the band. The problem was that the best round-aperture
designs only worked at exactly their nominal dimensions, and small changes dropped performance
quickly. The workshop can't hold dimensions that tightly, so I ran a Monte Carlo tolerance study
and switched to a more forgiving aperture.

That aperture was the **X-slot**. It gives up a little peak performance for a flatter response
across the machining tolerance range, and it became the final geometry:

### Physical design and manufacturing

Once the geometry was fixed, a mechanical engineering team member turned it into a CAD model and
manufacturing plan. The coupler is made from aluminium L-sections and sheet, with the
X-apertures laser-cut or CNC-milled into the shared broad wall, then welded and faced.
CONN003-W coaxial connectors mount on the top face:

<Figure
  src="/projects/rf-coupler-coax/coupler-cad-render.png"
  alt="CAD render of the Moreno cross-guide WR340 directional coupler top view showing two X-apertures in the common wall and two coaxial connectors protruding from the top"
  caption="CAD render of the Moreno coupler (designed by a mechanical engineering team member). The two X-apertures in the common broad wall and the two CONN003-W coaxial connectors (top) are visible."
  width={1284}
  height={1046}
/>

The manufacturing steps come straight from the tolerance study: laser-cut or CNC-milled
X-apertures, a CNC-slotted joint where the two waveguide bodies cross, 11.7 mm connector holes
drilled to the drawing, and sheet sections folded and TIG-welded. The step the Monte Carlo runs
showed mattered most was milling the weld faces at the aperture region flat to ±0.2 mm, since
that's the tolerance needed to stay inside the simulated coupling spread.

We also worked out with the manufacturer to run the welds along the centre of each face instead
of at the corners. A corner weld pulls as it cools and distorts the 90° bend, while a centre weld
leaves the folded corners alone, so the internal cross-section stays closer to the WR340
dimensions the simulation assumed.

### VNA characterisation plan

The simulation is only a prediction, so the next step is to measure the built coupler against
the −40 dB target. A directional coupler has four ports but a standard VNA measures two at a
time, so the unused ports are terminated in matched 50 Ω loads and several two-port measurements
are combined to cover every port pair.

<Figure
  src="/projects/rf-coupler-coax/vna-coupler-under-test.png"

  caption="The mainline measurement. The coupler sits between two WR340 waveguide-to-coax adapters, which is the only way to get its waveguide ports onto the VNA's coax ports."

#### De-embedding the test fixtures

The two adapters are part of every measurement, and each one adds its own loss, phase shift and
reflection. Removing them takes two steps.

**SOLT calibration** (Short, Open, Load, Thru) comes first. It moves the VNA reference planes to
the ends of the coax cables, so the cables and connectors drop out of the measurement:

<Figure
  src="/projects/rf-coupler-coax/vna-calibration-plane.png"
  alt="Diagram of a two-port VNA with arrows marking the calibration reference plane at the ends of the two coaxial test cables"
  caption="SOLT calibration moves the reference plane from inside the VNA out to the cable ends. Everything past that point still has to be removed by de-embedding."
  width={1649}
  height={954}
  wide
/>

**De-embedding** then removes the adapters. A single adapter can't be measured by itself because
one side is waveguide, not coax. Instead the two adapters are bolted back-to-back through their
WR340 flanges and measured as one two-port network:

<Figure
  src="/projects/rf-coupler-coax/vna-adapter-deembed.png"
  alt="Measurement setup diagram: two WR340 waveguide-to-coax adapters bolted together flange-to-flange, with their coax ports connected to a two-port VNA"
  caption="The two adapters bolted flange-to-flange. Measuring the pair is what makes a single adapter recoverable."
  width={1621}
  height={970}
  wide
/>

In cascade (ABCD) matrices, which multiply when networks are chained, the back-to-back
measurement is

$$
M_{AB} = M_T \cdot M_T
$$

where $M_T$ is one adapter. Since the two are identical, a single adapter is the matrix square
root:

$$
M_T = \sqrt{M_{AB}}
$$

With $M_T$ known, the adapters are removed from each coupler measurement by matrix inversion. The
mainline measurement has an adapter on both ports, $M_{\text{meas}} = M_T \, M_{\text{DUT}} \, M_T$,
so the coupler alone is

$$
M_{\text{DUT}} = M_T^{-1} \, M_{\text{meas}} \, M_T^{-1}
$$

A coupling or isolation measurement only has an adapter on the driven port, so one of the two
factors drops out. Converting $M_{\text{DUT}}$ back to S-parameters gives the coupler referenced
at its own ports.

#### Target parameters

The four parameters that define the coupler's performance:

| Parameter | Physical meaning | Simulation target |
|-----------|-----------------|-------------------|
| S(3,1) | Coupling factor | −40 dB |
| S(4,1) | Isolation | < −65 dB |
| S(2,1) | Through-path insertion loss | < 0.1 dB |
| S(1,1) | Input return loss | < −25 dB |

If the measured values fall inside the Monte Carlo tolerance bands, the simulation model is
validated, and we know the coupling factor well enough to convert a detector voltage back into
main-line watts.

## Waveguide-to-coax transition

The sampled power leaves the coupler's coupled port still in WR340 waveguide, travelling in the
**TE₁₀** mode, but the attenuator and detector after it use coax. The transition converts the
waveguide TE₁₀ mode into the **TEM** mode that 50 Ω coax carries.

I used a **broad-wall probe**: the coax centre conductor goes into the waveguide through a Teflon
insulator in the broad wall. A **back-short** (a short-circuit wall behind the probe) reflects the
incident wave back so both contributions reach the probe tip in phase. Tuning the probe depth and
back-short distance together brings the coax-port input impedance to 50 Ω at 2.45 GHz.

## Power measurement PCB

The last step is turning the coax signal into a number a computer can read. A Schottky diode
detector would work, but its dynamic range is narrow and it drifts with temperature, which isn't
good for something feeding a control loop. We used the **AD8317 logarithmic detector IC**
instead. It works from 1 MHz to 10 GHz and outputs a DC voltage that's linear in dBm across a
50 dB range, to within ±1 dB.

The log-linear output keeps the software simple. Because $V_{\text{out}}$ is linear in
$P_{\text{in}}$ (dBm), the Raspberry Pi can convert an ADC reading to power with one straight-line
equation instead of a lookup table.

A **15 dB inline attenuator** sits before the AD8317 to bring the coupled power (around −10 dBm
at 1 kW main-line power with −40 dB coupling) into the IC's best range. We also considered the
**ADL5330** variable attenuator, but the fixed passive attenuator was simpler and has predictable
loss at 2.45 GHz.

### Version 2

A teammate redesigned the board, building on the V1 design. The
circuit is the standard AD8317 measurement-mode setup. The RF input is terminated with a 52.3 Ω
resistor (R1) and AC-coupled into INHI/INLO through two 47 nF capacitors (C1, C2). VSET is tied
back to VOUT so the chip works as a power detector. R7 (8.2 kΩ) on TADJ sets the temperature
compensation, close to the 8 kΩ the datasheet recommends around 2.2 GHz. The 3.3 V supply is
decoupled with 100 nF and 100 pF (C4, C5), and VOUT goes out through a 100 Ω / 10 nF filter
(R6, CL).

  src="/projects/rf-coupler-coax/pcb-v2-schematic.png"

The main changes from V1 are in the layout:

- **Grounded coplanar waveguide (GCPW) RF input.** The trace from the edge-mount SMA to the chip
  has ground on both sides and on the layer below. The calculated impedance is 50.61 Ω, which was
  the closest to 50 Ω the stackup allowed.
- **Via fences** along both sides of the RF trace, plus via stitching across the whole ground
  pour, to keep RF noise from coupling in or out.
- **FR-4 dielectric**, the same as V1. FR-4 has higher loss at high frequency but is still
  usable up to about 3 GHz, and the board is small enough that the trace loss isn't noticeable.
  Rogers and Teflon boards from JLCPCB and PCBWay have lower loss, but they cost around AU$140
  and AU$100, they're limited to 2 layers, and they have other size limits. That wasn't worth it
  for a trace this short.
- **No RF shield can** over the chip. My teammate couldn't find a suitable size sold separately,
  so it's something to add in a future version.

### Bench test

We tested V2 using a **tinySA Ultra** as a signal generator. It output a 2.45 GHz CW signal into
the board's RF input, and we stepped the level in 10 dB steps while reading VOUT on a bench
multimeter. The AD8317 output goes down as input power goes up, so a working board should show
VOUT dropping by about 22 mV for every 1 dB increase.

<Video
  src="/projects/rf-coupler-coax/pcb-v2-test.mp4"
  poster="/projects/rf-coupler-coax/pcb-v2-test-poster.jpg"
  caption="The bench test: the tinySA set to 2.45 GHz at −48.5, −38.5, −28.5 and −18.5 dBm, with VOUT on the multimeter at each step."
/>

| tinySA output | Measured VOUT | Datasheet typical (2.2 GHz) |
|---------------|---------------|-----------------------------|
| −48.5 dBm | 1.281 V | 1.375 V |
| −38.5 dBm | 1.034 V | 1.155 V |
| −28.5 dBm | 0.800 V | 0.935 V |
| −18.5 dBm | 0.655 V | 0.715 V |

The datasheet doesn't list 2.45 GHz, so the comparison line uses the closest specified frequency,
2.2 GHz, where the typical slope is −22 mV/dB and the intercept is 14 dBm
($V_{\text{out}} = -0.022\,(P_{\text{in}} - 14)$).

<DetectorChart caption="Measured VOUT against the AD8317 typical transfer function at 2.2 GHz. Hover a point for its values." />

The board works. Between −48.5 and −28.5 dBm the output falls by 24.0 mV/dB on average, close to
the 22 mV/dB typical slope, so the detector is responding the way it should. The measured
voltages sit 0.06–0.14 V below the datasheet line. The datasheet intercept is a typical value at
a different frequency, and the tinySA's output level isn't calibrated, so an offset like this is
expected. The last step (−28.5 to −18.5 dBm) only dropped 14.5 mV/dB. We haven't worked out yet
whether that's the board or the tinySA.

For the real system we'll calibrate the board against a known reference source at 2.45 GHz and
use that line instead of the datasheet numbers.
