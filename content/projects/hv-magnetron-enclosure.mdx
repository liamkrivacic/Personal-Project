A magnetron needs a few kilovolts of DC to run. For AtomCraft's RF heating system that's around
4 kV, so the supply had to be safe for students to work on as well as hitting the right voltage.
This page covers the circuit design and simulation, the enclosure, and the safety procedures.

<Callout title="Design scope">
This is **simulation and design work**: LTspice circuit simulation, the Fusion 360 enclosure and
the safety procedure planning. It's the full design of the supply, not a commissioned and
validated build.
</Callout>

## Circuit design

The goal was roughly 4 kV DC from a standard mains input. I used a voltage-multiplying rectifier
after the high-voltage transformer, which lets a roughly 2 kV transformer secondary reach the
target voltage instead of needing a transformer that supplies the full 4 kV by itself. On a
student project that gets moved around a lab, the weight and cost of the transformer matter.

### The voltage doubler

I modelled the post-transformer side of the supply in LTspice as a 2 kV peak AC source, so the
schematic starts after the step-up transformer and the mains input and transformer aren't part of
this simulation. The first circuit was a half-wave voltage doubler. On one half-cycle the
capacitor charges from the transformer secondary, and on the other half-cycle the capacitor
voltage adds to the source, pushing the output toward roughly twice the 2 kV peak (before load,
diode drop and ripple).

I simulated the charge and discharge behaviour before choosing any component values, since
mistakes are much cheaper to find in a simulator than on a charged capacitor bank:

### Full-wave voltage doubler

The half-wave doubler works, but the ripple was higher than I wanted and it draws current in
pulses. The second circuit I modelled was a full-wave voltage doubler. It uses the same 2 kV peak
transformer-secondary model, but two diodes conduct on alternate half-cycles, each charging its
own capacitor. The output is taken across both capacitors in series, which charges more
continuously than the half-wave circuit. The simulation showed lower ripple and faster charging
than the half-wave case with the same filter capacitance:

The main hazard here is the energy stored in the capacitor bank. At 4 kV the capacitors hold
enough energy to kill someone long after the mains has been switched off and the supply looks
dead, which is why the safety framework treats capacitor discharge as a verified, enforced step.

## RF control circuit

Running a 1 kW magnetron in a university lab means the operator shouldn't control the
high-voltage supply directly. There needs to be a controller in between that blocks anything
unsafe. I led the research that set the architecture for that interlock chain, built around an
**ESP32-WROOM-32E** as the main controller. It takes operator commands over USB serial, checks the
interlock state, and only turns on the high-voltage enable when every condition is met. A
teammate then designed the control-system PCB based on that research.

I'm currently designing a high-voltage op-amp board that measures the supply's output voltage and
provides isolation between the high-voltage side and mains.

The main rule is that there's no conductive path anywhere between the low-voltage control side
and the 4 kV rail. Every signal that crosses between them goes through **optical isolation rated
to 5000 V**. Each interlock input and the enable output goes through an optocoupler, so a fault
on the high-voltage side can't reach the ESP32 or the operator's laptop on the other end of the
USB cable.

The switching uses **solid-state relays** instead of mechanical contactors. They have no moving
parts, no contact bounce and no spark, whereas a mechanical contactor closing onto a capacitively
loaded high-voltage rail can arc.

### I/O summary

| Signal | Direction | Isolation | Function |
|--------|-----------|-----------|----------|
| HV Enable | Output | 5000 V optocoupler | Arms the solid-state relay to energise the supply |
| Interlock 1 | Input | 5000 V optocoupler | Cabinet door closed |
| Interlock 2 | Input | 5000 V optocoupler | Capacitor discharge confirmed |
| Interlock 3 | Input | 5000 V optocoupler | Over-voltage trip cleared |
| Interlock 4 | Input | 5000 V optocoupler | Reflected power within safe limit (from RF monitoring system) |
| Temperature | Input | Isolated ADC | Transformer thermal monitor |
| Serial | I/O | USB (galvanic) | Operator command interface |

The ESP32 runs a small state machine with one rule: HV Enable is only on while all five
interlock inputs are active, and if any one of them drops, the enable turns off immediately,
regardless of what the operator has entered.

### Reflected power interlock

The RF system has a directional coupler and a power detector circuit that monitors both forward
and reflected power on the transmission line (covered in the RF coupler case study). One output
of that system is a voltage proportional to reflected power, and that signal feeds into the
ESP32 interlock chain as **Interlock 4**.

If the plasma load becomes badly mismatched (because the stub tuner hasn't caught up, the vessel
is outside its expected operating range, or there's a fault on the line), reflected power
spikes. Once it passes a set threshold, Interlock 4 drops, the ESP32 sees the trip, and HV
Enable turns off. The magnetron shuts down before sustained reflected power can heat
its output waveguide, damage its internal vanes or cause RF leakage from a mismatched section of
the line.

This connects the monitoring system to the power supply, so the directional coupler is part of
the safety chain as well as a diagnostic.

## Enclosure design in Fusion 360

I designed the enclosure in Fusion 360 as a stainless-steel sheet-metal part, including the
flat pattern for fabrication, ventilation cutouts sized for the transformer and capacitor heat,
a power-entry cutout, and removable access panels for inspection and maintenance.

The internal layout was based on safety. The transformer sits at the base so the centre of mass
is low and the unit is stable, the capacitor bank is kept away from the access panel so you don't
reach past stored energy during routine work, and cabling is routed away from hot surfaces and
sharp edges. I also put together a parts list so it could be ordered and built.

## Safety framework

Most of the decisions above were already safety decisions, so the framework mostly writes them
down explicitly. It covers:

- **Mains isolation**: a lockable isolator on the incoming supply, so the cabinet cannot be
  energised while the access panel is open.
- **Capacitor discharge**: a bleed resistor across the capacitor bank and a mandatory
  discharge-and-verify step before any internal access. This step is enforced by Interlock 2
  in the control circuit: the HV Enable cannot be re-asserted until the discharge is
  confirmed.
- **Over-voltage protection**: a crowbar circuit monitors the DC rail and trips the supply
  if the voltage exceeds the safe operating limit. The trip clears Interlock 3 until
  manually reset.
- **Procedural controls**: short-pulse operation protocols to limit the energy in the
  transmission line during experiments; safe re-entry procedures that require two-person
  confirmation before restoring power after an interlock trip.
- **Reflected power protection**: if reflected power on the transmission line exceeds a safe
  threshold, the RF monitoring system drives Interlock 4 and trips HV Enable before sustained
  reflected power can damage the magnetron or cause RF leakage from a mismatched line section.
- **Physical barriers**: the enclosure isolates all high-voltage conductors from the
  operator surface; no bare conductors are within reach of a standard access panel opening.

The general approach was to not rely on people being careful, and to make the safe way of doing
things also the easiest. The enclosure is laid out so that opening the cabinet takes the operator
through a safe state first, and skipping the procedure is more work than following it.
