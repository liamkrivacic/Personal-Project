As part of the RF team at AtomCraft, I designed a small board that measures the voltage on the
high-voltage capacitor in our magnetron power supply. It's a safety part. Before anyone opens the
enclosure, the control system needs to know the capacitor has actually discharged, rather than
assuming the bleed resistors did their job. The board turns that check into a measurement, and
it keeps the high-voltage side electrically isolated from the low-voltage control side.

<Callout title="Design status">
This page covers the schematic and PCB layout, done in Altium Designer. The board hasn't been
tested yet.
</Callout>

| Parameter | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| Measurement range | ±6.2 kV at the capacitor |
| Divider | 2 × 50 MΩ (Stackpole HVA12) + 16.2 kΩ (0.1%), ratio ≈ 1/6174 |
| Divider current | about 20 µA at 2 kV |
| Isolated amplifier | TI AMC3330, ±1 V input, 6 kV peak reinforced isolation |
| Isolation gap | at least 8 mm between the high- and low-voltage sides |
| HV clearance (IPC-2221) | 25 mm at the full-voltage input, 12.5 mm at the divider midpoint |
| Board size | 85 × 95 mm, 2 layers |

## How it works

The capacitor voltage is scaled down by a resistive divider made of two 50 MΩ resistors in series
with a 16.2 kΩ resistor. That gives a ratio of about 1/6174, so the ±1 V input range of the
amplifier corresponds to about ±6.2 kV at the capacitor. That leaves plenty of headroom above the
roughly 2 kV the capacitor sits at in normal operation, and the divider only draws about 20 µA.

For the top of the divider I used Stackpole HVA12 resistors, which are rated to 8 kV each. My
first plan was ordinary 0.25 W resistors, until I realised that at high voltage the limit is the
resistor's voltage rating, not its power. At 20 µA the power is tiny, but a standard resistor
would arc across long before it got warm. Splitting the top of the divider across two resistors
also halves the voltage each one sees, which keeps them well within their rating. The bottom
(sense) resistor is a 0.1% part, and the board gets a one-off calibration to account for resistor
tolerance and voltage coefficient.

The divided signal goes through ferrite beads and a small filter capacitor into a TI AMC3330
isolated amplifier. I picked the AMC3330 because it has an integrated isolated DC/DC converter,
so the high-voltage side is powered from the low-voltage side and doesn't need its own isolated
supply. Its differential output and diagnostic pin go to a 6-pin connector that connects to an STM32
microcontroller. The STM32 relays the reading to our central PLC, which handles the door
interlock, and turns on a light that shows whether the capacitor has discharged.

## Layout and safety

The board is split into a high-voltage side and a low-voltage side, with the AMC3330 bridging the
isolation barrier. The two grounds are kept completely separate, no copper crosses under the chip,
and there's at least 8 mm between the two sides.

I set the high-voltage clearances using the IPC-2221 guideline for uncoated external conductors,
which works out to about 0.005 mm per volt above 500 V. That gave 25 mm of clearance around the
full-voltage input and 12.5 mm around the divider's midpoint. Air itself breaks down at roughly 1
to 3 kV per mm, but distances across a board surface need a lot more margin, because dust and
moisture can slowly build up a conductive track. To fit these clearances I made the board
85 × 95 mm, which still fits in the cheapest 100 × 100 mm fabrication tier.

The rest of the layout follows the same idea:

- The divider resistors are long-body through-hole parts laid out in a line, stepping down in
  voltage away from the input.
- The high-voltage input uses two separate single-pole terminals placed far apart, instead of a
  standard two-pin block where the pins would be a few millimetres from each other.
- The ground pour is limited to the low-voltage output side instead of flooding the whole board,
  so there's no grounded copper near the high-voltage parts.
- The mounting holes are non-plated and kept well away from the high-voltage parts.

The PCB material insulates well, but the solder joints on the bottom of the high-voltage parts
are exposed metal sitting a few millimetres above a grounded chassis. To deal with that, the board
mounts on nylon standoffs above an acrylic insulating sheet, with Kapton tape over the
high-voltage joints.

## What I learned

Designing for high voltage changed how I approached the board. The layout was driven more by
spacing and insulation than by the circuit itself, and the main risks came from physical details
like exposed solder joints and the board surface between parts. On the tooling side, I learned to
use standard IPC footprints and generic passives instead of trusting downloaded parts, after
several came with wrongly sized footprints.
