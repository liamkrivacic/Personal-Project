AtomCraft is a student engineering project building a kilowatt-level microwave heating system for
a plasma test vessel. I'm the RF Engineer and RF Team Lead, which means I'm responsible for the RF
chain (everything between the magnetron and the vessel) and I run the nine-person team building
it. The RF chain is the highest-risk part of the build.

<Callout title="Team scope">
Leading a **9-person cross-disciplinary RF team** across simulation, power monitoring, impedance
matching, safety compliance and experiment planning. The system is moving toward final
commissioning.
</Callout>

## The transmission line

The system runs at 2.45 GHz in WR340 waveguide. The line goes from the magnetron to the plasma
vessel through:

**Magnetron → waveguide adaptor → isolator / dummy load → directional coupler → three-stub
tuner → RF port window → Vlasov antenna → vessel**

The isolator protects the magnetron from reflected power, the coupler samples forward and
reflected power, the stub tuner re-matches the line as the plasma load changes, and the RF window
seals the vacuum vessel while letting the microwave power through.

## What I did

- Led the HFSS simulation work for the directional coupler, waveguide-to-coax transition and
  three-stub tuner (see the individual project pages).
- Reviewed subsystem designs from team members and coordinated integration between the
  electrical, mechanical and vacuum subgroups.
- Wrote the safety compliance documentation: exposure limits, interlock requirements, personnel
  protection procedures and experiment protocols.
- Ran weekly team updates, split the work into packages, and handled recruitment and onboarding
  as the team grew.

## Safety

High-power 2.45 GHz work has a real RF exposure risk. Writing the compliance documents ended up
driving a lot of the design, because the requirements decided where components could go, which
panels had to be interlocked and what the experiment startup sequence had to be. Every experiment
also has to go through an approval process before it can run, so I've written a lot of RMFs and
SWPs.

## What I learned

Leading a technical team is a different skill from doing the technical work yourself. Most of
the job is keeping the design consistent across subsystems and people: catching a decision in the
electrical group before it causes a problem for the vacuum group, sizing work packages properly,
and giving people enough context to work without me checking on them. I've learned that on a
multi-year build with real hardware and real safety requirements.
